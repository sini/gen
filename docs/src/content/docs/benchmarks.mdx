Every performance gate in this ecosystem reads a deterministic evaluator counter — thunks
created, allocation, function calls. None reads cpu. That's not a missing feature, it's the
conclusion of a measurement. On this project's own benchmarking hardware, the pure-vs-reference
cpu ratio moved **0.734–1.873×** across repeated runs of byte-identical trees, while every counter
on those same runs stayed byte-identical (`ci/README.md`, CPU frequency scaling). A number that
swings by a factor of 2.5 on work that didn't change is not a signal — it's the host's frequency
governor, measured, not assumed. Counters don't have that problem: they're a function of the
expression alone, not of the expression and the machine's mood.

Every figure on this page carries the date or revision it was measured at. None of them are live
HEAD numbers — they're dated snapshots, and they say so.

## What's gated, and on what

All three performance gates — **parity**, **ratio**, **linearity** — read deterministic counters.
Parity requires every cell's output digest to match across stacks first, so a fast-but-wrong
change fails before its timing is even considered. Ratio requires the pure stack to build lighter
than the reference stack on thunks and allocation at the largest workload size. Linearity requires
those counters to grow no worse than linearly across a ×4 size step — the net that once caught a
real quadratic bug in a key-union helper. cpu is measured and printed beside all three, and gates
none of them.

The mechanical detail — which CI job runs this, and how it fails the build — is
[CI and checks](/reference/ci/). This page is the numbers, and what they do and don't support.

## Composition-plane results

Pure-gen vs. the frozen nixpkgs stack it replaced, on den's own composition shapes, from the
**2026-07-04 module-system audit** — isolated composition shapes, terminal-plane closure
evaluation excluded:

| workload | n | nixpkgs | pure-gen | speedup | thunks× | alloc× |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| aspects | 1600 | 0.353s | 0.126s | 2.80× | 2.99× | 3.60× |
| registry | 2000 | 0.165s | 0.073s | 2.27× | 2.20× | 2.64× |
| lazyRegistry | 2000 | 0.165s | 0.077s | 2.14× | 2.20× | 2.64× |
| schemaHosts | 1600 | 0.223s | 0.126s | 1.77× | 1.75× | 2.04× |
| scalar | 16000 | 0.182s | 0.114s | 1.59× | 1.27× | 1.52× |
| startup | 1 | 11.6ms | 7.7ms | 1.5× | — | — |

`thunks×`/`alloc×` are nixpkgs ÷ pure-gen — higher means pure-gen allocates less. Scaling is
linear on both stacks; pure-gen's per-item slope is lower on every row measured here. gen-merge's
engine (936 lines) reimplements what nixpkgs' `modules.nix` plus `types.nix` (4225 lines) does,
over the surface den exercises. The same audit caught a real quadratic key-union bug that the
linearity gate now guards against permanently.

Two dedicated workloads carry their own tighter gate: gen-class's fixed-input reconstruction
builds roughly **0.17×** the full re-merge's thunk graph, and gen-merge's warm re-eval builds
roughly **0.17×** the cold path's thunks and allocation — both byte-identical to the path they
shortcut, both permanently gated at ≤ 0.30×.

### What observability costs

gen-merge's always-on provenance channel — the machinery behind [Trust](/trust/)'s observability
promise — is not free, and the cost is measured rather than assumed: **+3.7–6.3% pure-side
thunks** across the workload set (scalar 0.844→0.882 ratio-to-ref, registry 0.493→0.524), measured
against gen-merge `11b39d7`, still comfortably inside every ratio gate. Observability is a channel
paid for once, up front, not per item read from it.

## Compat mode

A hybrid stack — the gen-merge engine driven by **real** nixpkgs option types instead of
gen-schema's own — runs the same corpus byte-identically, because nixpkgs option types already
speak the same merge contract gen-merge implements. Leaf-type shims are free; structural-type
shims (`submodule`) give the whole engine-side win back, because a nixpkgs submodule's own
`.merge` calls `lib.evalModules` per instance. Compat mode is a migration and escape-hatch tool,
not a way to run the ecosystem fast.

## The 3-way comparison, told straight

<Aside type="caution" title="Read this before quoting a speedup">
On one of the three benchmarks in this comparison, gen-lib is the **slowest of the three
frameworks on wall clock** — while carrying the **lowest** deterministic function-call, prim-op,
and thunk counts of the three on every single benchmark, including that one. Both facts are true,
from the same run, and this page reports both rather than the one that reads better.
</Aside>

One representative flake (three packages, one devShell, one formatter, × 3 systems) expressed
three ways — flake-parts, adios-flake, and the hub's own `gen.lib` — under the same methodology,
the same pinned nixpkgs, 5 reps per cell. Re-baselined against the hub at rev `8dd9fda`
(2026-08-26):

| Benchmark | flake-parts | adios-flake | gen-lib |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| packages.x86_64-linux (1 system) | 0.186s | 0.366s | 0.332s |
| formatter (3 systems) | 0.916s | 0.900s | 0.397s |
| devShells (3 systems) | 0.503s | 0.491s | **0.822s** |

gen-lib carries the lowest function-call, prim-op, and thunk counts of the three on every one of
those rows — including the devShells row it loses on wall clock. Both facts are sourced from the
same table. The deltas here are small next to a published **28–30%** cpu figure from adios-flake's
own benchmark of a much larger, real 36-input flake — that isn't a fair comparison to draw,
because the workload shapes differ: a small synthetic flake like this one is dominated by the
shared nixpkgs terminal every variant pays equally, diluting whatever the framework itself
contributes.

What actually holds across all five outputs, all three systems, all three frameworks: **drvPath
equivalence, 15 for 15.** Every output this comparison builds is byte-identical regardless of
which framework built it. That's the oracle this comparison is really for — the counter table
describes engine overhead on an already-proven-identical result, not a different, cheaper answer.

## Fleet-scale results

The numbers above are self-contained in this hub's own CI, on isolated composition shapes.
Fleet-scale numbers are measured somewhere else, deliberately:
**[hola](https://github.com/sini/hola)**, a separate public repository, forces a real three-host
fleet pinned by a separately versioned config repo, under the same deterministic-counter
discipline, byte-gated first. This hub's own CI does not re-run that measurement — its
`fleet-consistency` check only re-derives the arithmetic behind already-committed baseline
numbers.

All figures below were green at hola `d643a8d` (CI run `28727988245`), against the fleet pinned by
nix-config `8f84aa6`:

- Composition is **5.2%** of the terminal's `//`-merge storm, fleet-wide.
- A localized single-host edit skips **66.7%** of fleet composition, floored at a permanent
  ≥ 60% gate.
- Injecting a byte-identical shared systemd-units core saves roughly **1.6%** function calls and
  **0.18%** `//`-copies per added fleet member, floored at ≥ 0.8%.
- A class-share arm carries **+4.6%** overhead, byte-sound but never floored as a win — a cost
  that's measured and reported rather than hidden because it didn't help.

<Aside type="tip" title="Not every number here is a win, on purpose">
That last row is in this table because this page reports what was measured, not what flatters the
result. A regression-gate suite that only ever prints wins isn't measuring anything.
</Aside>
